# The craft of excellent nonfiction

A research report on what excellent writers of essays, arguments, analysis, and commentary actually do differently, and a working system built from it. It sits underneath every style profile in `style/`. A style decides *how the piece sounds*. This file covers what makes a piece *good* whatever it sounds like. When the two conflict on a matter of expression (sentence length, first person, humor, headers), the named style wins. On matters of reasoning and honesty, neither overrides the vault invariants in `CLAUDE.md`.

**How to read the labels.** Every principle is tagged by where its authority comes from:

- **[EMP]** empirical research finding, with a strength rating (strong / moderate / weak / contested)
- **[RP]** established rhetorical, logical, or linguistic principle (theory, not experiment)
- **[CONV]** professional convention (editors, writing centers, newsrooms)
- **[ADV]** advice from a named individual writer or editor
- **[SYN]** my own synthesis across sources

**Access levels.** The research was done on 2026-09-29 by five parallel research passes. Each source in the bibliography is marked by what was actually examined: *full text*, *excerpt*, *abstract*, or *secondary* (someone else's summary, or a page summarizer). Quotations marked *(secondary)* should be checked against the original before being repeated in anything published.

**A caution about the empirical research.** Almost all of it tests undergraduates or schoolchildren reading short passages, usually science or health material, and measures recall or attitude shortly afterwards. None of it tests long-form essays read voluntarily by adult general readers. The findings are good evidence about *mechanisms* and weak evidence about *magnitudes* for this genre. Where I extrapolate, I say so.

**The illustrative examples** ("Weak / Improved") are mine. The claims inside them are invented for demonstration and are not facts.

## 1. Ideas and thesis

### 1.1 The ladder: topic → question → problem → thesis → argument → insight

These words get used loosely. Kept distinct, they diagnose most weak pieces.

- **Topic:** a subject area. "Free will in Tolstoy." Booth, Colomb & Williams: "A topic is probably too broad if you can state it in four or five words." [RP]
- **Observation:** something true you noticed. It is not yet a claim that anyone would contest.
- **Question:** what you want to find out about the topic.
- **Problem:** a question that has a *cost*. Booth, Colomb & Williams define a problem as a **condition plus a cost**: "A question raises a problem if not answering it keeps us from knowing something more important than its answer." Their counterexample is Lincoln's thumb against his nose. The question can be answered, but nothing turns on the answer. [RP, *Craft of Research*, full text of chs. 3–4]
- **Thesis:** your answer to the problem. Harvey: it must be "(a) true, but (b) arguable — not obviously or patently true — and (c) limited enough in scope to be argued in a short composition and with available evidence," and it should "get to the heart of the text or topic" and "govern the whole essay." [CONV/RP]
- **Argument:** the reasoned case that the thesis is right, including its answers to objections.
- **Insight:** the thing the reader could not have seen before and can now see. It is usually what a thesis is *for*. Graham's test: "Useful writing tells people something true and important that they didn't already know, and tells them as unequivocally as possible." [ADV]

**Diagnostic: the three-step formula** [RP, Booth/Colomb/Williams]:
> I am studying ___ (topic), because I want to find out ___ (question), in order to help my reader understand ___ (significance).

If you can't fill the third blank, you have a question without a problem. "Nothing. I just want to know" is, in their words, "good enough to start, but not to finish." Curiosity gets you started. It doesn't earn the piece a reader.

### 1.2 Motive: what makes a thesis interesting rather than merely correct

The most useful single concept in the research is Gordon Harvey's **motive** [RP/CONV, full text]:

> "A problem, difficulty, dilemma, over-simplification, misapprehension, or violated expectation that the writer defines at the start of the essay, and to which the essay's thesis acts as an answer, solution, correction, or clarification."

A thesis is interesting only *against* a background that makes it non-obvious. Motive supplies that background. Harvey notes it is "usually introduced by a form of the complicating word 'But.'" His list of motive types works as a generator for angles:

- the truth isn't what one would expect
- there's an interesting wrinkle or complexity
- the standard opinion needs challenging or qualifying
- there's a contradiction or tension
- there's an ambiguity
- there's a mystery or puzzle
- we can learn about a larger phenomenon by studying this smaller one
- a seemingly tangential or insignificant matter is actually important

The motive must be **genuine**: "a misapprehension or puzzle that an intelligent reader would plausibly have." If you invent a naive view nobody holds so that your thesis can correct it, the motive is a straw man.

Princeton's writing program splits motive into two sources [CONV]: an incongruity in the *material itself*, or a hole or disagreement in *other people's accounts* of it. Graff & Birkenstein's "They Say / I Say" model is the second kind made into a template [CONV, secondary only]. Its critics note that the templates can produce "a weak argument" with all the right moves in place. The moves supply motive. They don't supply substance.

### 1.3 Davis: interesting means denying *some* of the reader's assumptions

Murray Davis's "That's Interesting!" (1971) [RP, sociology of knowledge; conceptual, not experimentally tested]:

> "Interesting theories deny certain assumptions of their audience, while non-interesting theories affirm certain assumptions of their audience."

The basic form is "What seems to be X is in reality non-X." A claim can fail three ways:

- **"That's obvious"**: it affirms what the reader already assumes.
- **"That's irrelevant"**: it doesn't engage anything the reader assumes.
- **"That's absurd"**: it denies the reader's *whole* assumption-ground, so they can't take it seriously.

The target is a claim that denies **one** assumption the reader actually holds while leaving enough of their world in place for them to follow you.

**The mechanism has a corollary that matters:** interest is *relative to an audience*. The same claim bores specialists and surprises laypeople. Deciding who the reader is comes before deciding whether an idea is interesting.

**The counterweight** [EMP, moderate]: the Open Science Collaboration's replication project (2015) found that "surprising effects were less reproducible." If readers' priors are usually roughly right, then a claim that overturns them is more likely to be wrong. Tsang (2021) [ADV] argues that chasing interestingness corrupts research. **Synthesis [SYN]:** surprise is how you *detect* a candidate insight. It is not evidence for it. The more counterintuitive the thesis, the more support it owes the reader. Graham's formula keeps both requirements: true *and* surprising.

### 1.4 How good writers find something worth saying

- **Write from where you've thought longest.** Graham: "Anything you notice that surprises you, who've thought about the topic a lot, will probably also surprise a significant number of readers." [ADV] This works only if you really are practiced in the topic. A novice's surprise is a poor signal.
- **Look for the "But."** Scan your material for Harvey's motive types, especially tensions and contradictions. In a corpus of raw thoughts, these are often places where the author disagrees with themselves.
- **Find the governing assumption.** Harvey warns about keyterms that set up "a false or constraining opposition." His example is an essay that treats Orwell's "political" and "moral" writing as opposites. Often the original angle is to reject the dichotomy everyone else argues inside.
- **Narrow until the claim can be defended with the evidence you have.** Harvey's "limited enough in scope" and Graham's "It's easy to make a statement correct by making it vague" are the same warning from two sides. Broad claims are safe only because they say nothing.

### 1.5 Does this idea deserve a piece at all?

Tests that converge across sources [SYN]:

1. **The iterated so-what** (Booth/Colomb/Williams). Keep asking "So what?" until the answer names a cost the reader bears. If you run out before you get there, you have a topic.
2. **The Davis reaction** (§1.3). Predict whether a representative reader says "obvious," "irrelevant," "absurd," or "huh."
3. **The multiplication test** (Graham): true × important × novel × stated strongly. The factors multiply, so a zero anywhere kills the piece.
4. **The learning test** (Graham): "If you don't learn anything from writing an essay, don't publish it." [ADV]
5. **The editor's three questions** (Neel Patel, NYT opinion) [ADV, secondary]: what argument you'll make, why you are the one to make it, and why now.

### 1.6 A real disagreement: thesis-first or discovery-first?

- **Discovery-first.** Graham: "you can't begin with a thesis, because you don't have one, and may never have one." Essays should "aim for maximum surprise." Didion: "I write entirely to find out what I'm thinking." Sommers's experienced writers kept drafting "until I figure out what I want to say." [ADV; EMP for Sommers]
- **Thesis-first.** Harvey: the thesis should be "given early." Op-ed editors want a pitch that "stake[s] out a position." [CONV]

**Resolution [SYN]:** the two claims concern different things. Discovery-first describes the **drafting process** for exploratory essays. Thesis-first describes the **finished product** in argument genres. Even Graham requires "a way in" before starting, meaning a question with something at stake, and says an essay "has to come up with answers." Draft to discover, then revise so the finished piece reads as though the writer knew. Pinker concedes that the "knowing" pose of classic style is "a pretense, an imposture, a stance."

> **Weak**
> Social media has both positive and negative effects on how people understand politics.
>
> **Problem**
> It is true of nearly every technology and denies nothing anyone believes. It fails the Davis test ("obvious") and Graham's precision test (correct *because* vague). It names no mechanism, so there is nothing to argue.
>
> **Improved**
> The trouble with political argument online isn't that people only see their own side. It's that they mostly see the other side's *worst* members, chosen for them because those posts travel furthest. A reader can be well exposed to opposing views and still end up with a caricature of them.
>
> **Why it works**
> There's a motive ("the standard view is wrong": the filter-bubble assumption). It denies one assumption the reader holds while leaving the rest intact. It names a mechanism that can be checked. A reasonable person could dispute it, so it needs an argument.

## 2. Argument

### 2.1 The anatomy: claim, grounds, warrant, qualifier, rebuttal

Toulmin's model [RP, via Purdue OWL]:

- **Claim:** what you want the reader to accept.
- **Grounds:** the evidence.
- **Warrant:** the principle that connects the grounds to the claim, often left unstated.
- **Backing:** support for the warrant.
- **Qualifier:** how strongly the claim is asserted ("usually," "in most cases").
- **Rebuttal:** the conditions under which the claim wouldn't hold.

Booth, Colomb & Williams recast the last two as **acknowledgment and response** to a critical but fair reader [RP].

**The mechanism that makes this useful [SYN]:** most weak arguments fail at the **warrant**, not the grounds. The evidence is real, but the unstated principle that connects it to the claim is false, contested, or narrower than the claim. Once you state the warrant, the reader can check it.

**When not to spell it out:** in a short piece, spelling out every warrant is pedantic. Make explicit the warrants your audience would contest, and leave the shared ones implicit.

> **Weak**
> Cities that built bike lanes saw fewer traffic deaths. So every city should build bike lanes.
>
> **Problem**
> The grounds may be fine. The hidden warrant ("what worked in those cities will work anywhere, and the bike lanes caused the drop") carries all the weight and isn't defended. The qualifier is missing ("every").
>
> **Improved**
> In the cities that built protected lanes, deaths fell most on the streets that got the lanes, not citywide. That points to the lanes themselves rather than to a general trend. It also means the case is strongest for streets like those: wide, fast, and heavily cycled. For a quiet residential grid the evidence says much less.
>
> **Why it works**
> The warrant (the lanes caused the drop) is defended by the one piece of evidence that separates cause from trend. The qualifier comes from the evidence, not from reflex. The claim shrinks to what the grounds actually support, and it becomes more persuasive.

### 2.2 Most essay arguments are defeasible, so qualifications are part of the logic

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy distinguishes deductive arguments, where the premises necessitate the conclusion, from inductive and abductive ones, where the premises lend "some degree of support." It also notes that outside formal domains, "human reasoning tends to be deeply defeasible": new information can overturn the conclusion [RP]. Policy and ethical essays are mostly **conductive**. They "accumulate non-decisive reasons" (SEP, "Informal Logic").

Johnson's account of the **dialectical tier** holds that "convincing arguments must provide answers to reasonable objections" [RP]. On this view, answering objections isn't a rhetorical tactic. It is *part of what makes an argument good*.

The practical consequence [SYN]: in a defeasible argument, the qualifier and the reply to the rebuttal are load-bearing. An essay that leaves them out isn't more confident. It is incomplete.

### 2.3 Acknowledging complexity makes an argument stronger, but only if you answer it

This is the most decision-relevant empirical finding in the research.

**O'Keefe 1999 meta-analysis** [EMP, moderate; full text]: 107 effect sizes, N = 20,111.
- Overall, one-sided and two-sided messages didn't differ (r = −.001). That null average hides two opposite effects:
- **Refutational** two-sided messages, which raise the opposing view and answer it, beat one-sided messages (r ≈ .08).
- **Non-refutational** two-sided messages, which mention the other side without answering it, were *less* persuasive than one-sided messages (r ≈ −.05).
- Outside advertising, refutational messages gained both credibility and persuasiveness.
- Audience starting position and education didn't substantially moderate the effect.
- The refutational advantage appeared chiefly when support and opposition were **interwoven**, not segregated into blocks.
- O'Keefe's own caveat: refuting "implausible minor objections" may not work like refuting serious ones.

**Allen 1991** replicates the direction [EMP, full text]: refutational r = .076, non-refutational r = −.060.

**Straw men** [EMP, Younis 2026, three experiments, full-text summary]: misrepresenting the opposing view lowered the writer's perceived trustworthiness (d ≈ −0.34) and reasonableness (d ≈ 0.5). It bought *no* measurable gain in persuasion.

**Limits:** the effects are small, the stimuli are short experimental messages, and the outcomes are immediate attitudes. What transfers is the **direction** of the effects, not their size.

**Principles [SYN]:**
1. Raising an objection and leaving it unanswered is **worse than not raising it at all**. It hands the reader a reason to reject you. The dutiful "To be sure…" paragraph that concedes and moves on is the failure case.
2. Answer the *strongest* objection, not the easiest. Rebutting weak objections signals that you couldn't handle strong ones (UNC: attacking a caricature "suggest[s] that your argument is only capable of defeating an extremely weak adversary").
3. **Weave** the objections in where they bite, instead of quarantining them in one paragraph near the end.
4. Deal with one or two serious counterarguments in depth rather than listing many superficially (UCC Skills Centre) [ADV].
5. If an objection is right, **change the claim**. "If discussing a counter argument changes your position, go back and reconsider your original argument." [ADV] Graham: "I don't try to fix the unconvincing bits by arguing more cleverly… More often than not I have to change what I was saying." [ADV]

> **Weak**
> Of course, some will say that a four-day week hurts productivity. But the benefits for workers are clear.
>
> **Problem**
> It is non-refutational. The objection is raised, then waved past with an assertion. By O'Keefe's evidence this is worse than silence, because the reader now holds a reason to disagree and no answer to it.
>
> **Improved**
> The strongest objection is productivity: if output falls by a fifth, the benefit to workers is paid for somewhere. The trials that tracked output, though, found it held roughly steady, largely because meetings shrank first. That answer covers office work with discretionary time. It says nothing about a nurse's shift, where hours *are* the output, and there the objection stands.
>
> **Why it works**
> It states the objection at full strength, answers it with a mechanism, and concedes precisely where the answer runs out. The concession narrows the claim instead of weakening it. The reader trusts the part that survives because they watched it being tested.

### 2.4 Critical questions: how to test each kind of reasoning

Walton treats argument schemes as defeasible patterns, each with its own **critical questions** [RP, full text of chapter]. A "fallacy" is a scheme that fails its critical questions, not a forbidden form (SEP, "Fallacies"; Hamblin's attack on textbook treatments as "debased, worn-out and dogmatic"). An appeal to authority, an analogy, or a slippery slope can be sound.

- **Expert opinion** (Walton's six): How credible is the expert? Is this their field? What exactly did they say? Are they personally reliable? Do other experts agree? Is the assertion based on evidence?
- **Causal claims:** what mechanism links A to B? What alternatives have you ruled out (confounds, reverse causation, chance)? UNC: "you should have something more to say about how A caused B than just that A came first."
- **Generalization:** how big and how varied is the sample, relative to how broad the claim is? "Broad claims need more proof than narrow ones." (UNC)
- **Analogy** (SEP, Bartha): the similarities must be *relevant to the conclusion*, and structural or causal rather than superficial. There must be no *critical* disanalogy. A weaker conclusion makes an analogy more plausible. An analogy is only as strong as the causal link that carries over from one case to the other.
- **Practical reasoning** (should we do X?): conflicting goals, alternative actions, feasibility, side effects.
- **Argument from ignorance** ("no evidence that…"): how thorough has the search been, and how thorough does it need to be?
- **Dichotomies:** are there really only two options, or do your key terms create the binary? (Harvey)

**Pragma-dialectics** adds the fair-play rules [RP]:
- An attack must address the standpoint that was actually advanced (the formal anti-straw-man rule).
- Don't disown your own unstated premises or invent your opponent's.
- Don't present a contested premise as agreed.
- A failed defense means you retract the standpoint.

### 2.5 Motivated reasoning is the default in the writing chair

Mercier & Sperber's argumentative theory [EMP-based theory, contested]: reasoning evolved to produce and evaluate arguments in dialogue. People are good at *evaluating* others' arguments and bad at evaluating their own. "Skilled arguers… are not after the truth but after arguments supporting their views." A solo writer is producing arguments for a position they already hold, which is exactly the mode in which myside bias is strongest.

**Practical remedy [SYN]:** simulate the evaluating side on purpose.
- Scott Alexander's **isolated-demands-for-rigor** check: would you accept this standard of evidence if it supported a conclusion you dislike? [ADV]
- Give the draft to a reader who disagrees.
- For each paragraph, ask what a hostile but fair critic would say.

## 3. Evidence

### 3.1 What needs evidence, and how much

- **Claims that need support:** anything the reader might reasonably doubt. That includes factual claims, causal claims, generalizations, and characterizations of what someone else believes. Shared knowledge and definitions don't need it.
- **How much** [CONV, Harvey]: "enough evidence to be persuasive; it needs to be the right kind of evidence… (with no obvious pieces of evidence overlooked); it needs to be sufficiently concrete for the reader to trust it…; and if summarized, it needs to be summarized accurately and fairly." The clause "no obvious pieces overlooked" is the anti-cherry-picking rule. If there's an obvious counterexample, you have to address it.
- **Scale with surprise [SYN]:** the more counterintuitive the claim (§1.3) and the broader it is, the more evidence it owes.
- **Journalism's version** [CONV, Kovach & Rosenstiel]: "DO NOT PRINT ONE IOTA BEYOND WHAT YOU KNOW." "Show your work so readers can decide for themselves why they should believe it." Most errors happen when writers "took something from somebody or someplace else and failed to check it themselves." That is the argument for going to primary sources.

### 3.2 Kinds of evidence and what each is good for

Hornikx distinguishes four types: **anecdotal** (a single case), **statistical** (numbers across many cases), **causal** (an explanation of mechanism), and **expert** (confirmation by an authority) [EMP].

- **Statistics vs. stories** [EMP, contested]:
  - Allen & Preiss (1997) found statistics more persuasive.
  - Zebregs et al. (2015) re-analysed the question by outcome type. **Statistics had the edge on beliefs** (d = −0.16). **Narratives had a marginal edge on intentions** (d = +0.10).
  - Their account: statistics act on cognitive responses, stories on affect.
  - The evidence base is small and mostly drawn from health topics.
- Lay readers expect statistical > expert > causal > anecdotal, and those expectations largely match actual persuasiveness (Hornikx 2008) [EMP].
- **An anecdote is a generalization from n = 1** [SYN from Hornikx]. The question "is this case representative?" applies to it and to statistics alike. When you use a case, say why it's typical, or say that it isn't and why it matters anyway.

**Working rule [SYN]:** use statistics or mechanism to establish *that* something is true, and use a case to show *what it is like* and why it matters. Don't let the vivid case carry a claim only the numbers can support.

- **Quotation** [CONV, UNC]: quote only when the wording itself matters: the source's exact phrasing is the evidence, or it's too good to paraphrase. Otherwise paraphrase and attribute.
- **Correlation and causation:** see the causal critical questions in §2.4. The honest move is to state the claim at the strength the evidence supports: "is associated with," "probably contributes to," "caused."

### 3.3 The chain: claim → evidence → interpretation → implication

UNC: "Evidence is always evidence for or against something, and you have to make that link clear." Do "at least as much analysis as you do evidence, or your paper runs the risk of being more summary than argument." [CONV] Harvey defines analysis as "saying what can be inferred from the data such that it supports a thesis." [RP]

Each piece of evidence should arrive inside a chain:
1. **Claim:** what this part of the argument asserts.
2. **Evidence:** the fact, case, quote, or number.
3. **Interpretation:** what the evidence shows, and why it counts as evidence for *this* claim. This is the warrant made local.
4. **Implication:** what follows for the larger argument. It is what makes the next paragraph necessary.

**Evidence dumping** is steps 1 and 2 repeated without steps 3 and 4. **Summary instead of analysis** is step 2 on its own.

> **Weak**
> Attendance at the city's libraries rose 40% after fines were abolished. Circulation rose 12%. Several librarians said patrons were returning. A 2019 study found similar results elsewhere.
>
> **Problem**
> It is four facts in a row. The reader can't tell what claim they support, why the numbers differ, or what follows. Citations have stood in for reasoning.
>
> **Improved**
> Abolishing fines brought people back, but not mainly to borrow: attendance rose 40% while circulation rose only 12%. The gap suggests the fines had been keeping people out of the *building*, not just deterring late returns. The people they had been keeping away were using the library as a place. If that's right, the case for abolishing fines rests less on book returns than on access to a public room, and it should be judged on those terms.
>
> **Why it works**
> The difference between the two numbers becomes the evidence, and the interpretation says what that difference shows. The implication changes the terms of the debate, which gives the next paragraph a job.

## 4. Structure

### 4.1 Structure is the order in which a reader needs things

The Harvard Writing Center's "Essay Structure" handout (Abrams, 2000) [CONV, secondary reproduction]: "Structuring your essay according to a reader's logic means examining your thesis and anticipating what a reader needs to know, and in what sequence." It divides the work into:

- **"What":** demonstrate the phenomenon. It should come early and take up no more than a third of the piece.
- **"How":** complication, meaning how the thesis stands up to a counterargument.
- **"Why":** the stakes. They belong at the end, and without them the essay reads as "unfinished — or, worse, as pointless or insular."

Harvey [RP]: sections should follow "a progressive order — there should be a direction of development or complication, not simply be a list or restatements of the thesis." The failure pattern: "Macbeth is ambitious: he's ambitious here; and he's ambitious here."

**What makes each paragraph make the next necessary [SYN]:** each unit ends by creating a need that the next unit meets. The need might be an implication that has to be tested, a complication that has to be answered, a question the evidence raises, or a claim that now needs a mechanism. A list has no such needs. You could shuffle its items and nothing would break. **The shuffle test:** if two paragraphs could swap places without anyone noticing, the structure is a list.

**The walk-through failure** (Abrams): the essay "follow[s] the structure of [its] sources rather than establishing [its] own." Watch for paragraphs that open with time words ("first," "next," "then") or listing words ("also," "another"). This is also the Spitball-specific risk in §1.4 of `CLAUDE.md`: retrieval order leaking into composition.

### 4.2 Structural patterns and when each fits

| Pattern | Shape | Fits when | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Claim → complication → refinement** | State it, test it against the hard case, restate it more exactly | Arguments where the interesting part is the qualification | Refinement that retreats into mush |
| **Problem → explanation → implication** | Here's a puzzle, here's why it happens, here's what follows | Analysis and explanatory commentary | Problem too small to carry the implication |
| **Question-driven** | Pose a real question, pursue it, answer it | Exploratory essays; Graham's model | Question with no answer at the end |
| **Situation → complication → question → answer** (Minto) [CONV, secondary] | Answer stated immediately after the setup | Readers who must decide quickly | Too flat-footed for reflective essays (Williams) |
| **Thesis → evidence → counterargument → synthesis** | The academic default | Contested claims for skeptical readers | Mechanical if the counterargument sits in a block (O'Keefe: weave it) |
| **Narrative / chronological** | Events in order, meaning drawn out as they happen | Pieces where process or change *is* the point | Walk-through; McPhee: "chronology traditionally wins" even when theme should |
| **Thematic / spiral** | Return to the same material at widening scope | Personal-historical essays (Baldwin) | Repetition without development |
| **Modular** | Sections that can be read somewhat independently | Long pieces, collections of observations | No cumulative argument |

These patterns are [SYN] built from Harvey, Abrams, Williams, Minto, McPhee, and the close readings in §14. None has empirical support over the others for essays. The journalism studies comparing inverted pyramid with narrative are **mixed** [EMP, weak]:
- Sternadori (2008) found no memory or enjoyment difference, with a marginal comprehension edge for the inverted pyramid.
- Ameseder et al. (2025/26, N = 461) found better comprehension and interest and lower load for narrative, but lower interest after the first paragraph.

McPhee [ADV, secondary]: "A piece of writing has to start somewhere, go somewhere, and sit down when it gets there." Structure should come from the material rather than being imposed on it (unverified quotation). There is also a tension between chronology and theme: "chronology traditionally wins," while "topics that have arisen now and again… cry out to be collected." McPhee writes narrative nonfiction, where chronology is the default. For argument, Abrams and Minto's reader-question model fits better.

### 4.3 When to reveal the thesis

There are three positions, and each is right somewhere [SYN]:

- **Early** (Minto; nut graf; Williams for "most readers in most professional contexts"). Use it when the reader has to decide quickly whether to invest, and for commentary, op-eds, and analysis.
- **Early, but not fully** (Harvey): "given early (not just be implied — though its fullest and sharpest statement may be withheld for a time)." Williams: "get them out early — but not immediately, not before you get to the end of a reasonably concise introductory issue." This is the default for most argumentative essays: state the claim clearly, and save its sharpest form for after the complication has earned it.
- **Late** (Williams's "belletristic essay," in which readers would find point-early organization "too crude, too flatfooted"; Branch's "Snow Fall"; Baldwin). Use it when the discovery *is* the value, or when the claim is so resisted that the reader has to be walked to it. Even here Williams insists on an **anticipatory point**: the opening must promise that a point is coming and name its themes.

Williams also names the bad reason for a late point: **"Failure to Revise."** The writer found the point at the end of drafting and never moved it. Diagnostic: "look first at the last sentence or two. It is there that you will often find the product of your thinking."

The journalists disagree about this too (Nieman Storyboard, 2019) [CONV]. Wells and DeGregory see the nut graf as "the promise of the piece." Branch hears it as the "'beep-beep-beep' of a truck in reverse" and a sign that "you don't trust the reader." Editors usually win that argument, and "the longer the piece, the less we rely on a nut graf."

### 4.4 Information hierarchy and pacing

- **Signals help readers remember what's signaled** [EMP, moderate to strong]. Lorch (1989): headings, previews, and summaries improve memory for the information they cue, "whereas memory for unsignaled information often is unaffected." Schneider et al. (2018 meta-analysis, secondary): retention g = 0.53. Signals redistribute attention. So signal what actually matters, and don't signal everything.
- **Signposting disagreement:**
  - Pinker: metadiscourse "is there to help the writer, not the reader," so use it "sparingly."
  - Klinkenborg: "Forget transitions."
  - Harvey: stitching, and roadmaps in long essays.
  - Minto: explicit hierarchy.
  - **Resolution [SYN]:** the more a piece has to be navigated (length, density, a reader looking for one part), the more explicit signaling it needs. Short essays read straight through can let the logic do the work. See the style profile for the local rule.
- **Pacing:** spend space in proportion to difficulty and importance, not to how much material you have. McPhee gathers "ten times as much stuff" as he uses [ADV]. The unused nine-tenths is what makes the used tenth well chosen.

## 5. Paragraphs

### 5.1 The topic-sentence doctrine vs. what professionals actually do

- **Textbook doctrine:** every paragraph opens with a topic sentence.
- **Braddock** (1974) [EMP, weak; secondary only] analysed 25 magazine essays. Only about 13% of paragraphs began with a topic sentence, about 3% ended with one, and only about 30% contained a simple explicit one at all.
- **Smith** (2008) [EMP, abstract] looked at history articles. A major topic sentence opened the *discourse block* (a multi-paragraph unit) in about two-thirds of cases.
- **Williams** [RP] rejects "topic sentence" because "good writers often introduce paragraphs with more than just a single sentence." He speaks instead of an **issue** (the opening segment) and a **discussion**.

**Resolution [SYN]:** professionals state the governing point at the head of a *unit of thought*, which may span several paragraphs. They don't mechanically open each indented block with a summary sentence. The principle that holds up is this: **the reader should know early what each unit is doing.** How that's signaled depends on the unit's size and the genre.

### 5.2 What makes a paragraph feel inevitable rather than assembled

Williams's cohesion principles [RP, excerpt] give the most concrete mechanism available:

1. **Topic strings.** The grammatical subjects of successive sentences refer to a small, consistent set of characters or concepts. They are consistent, "not identical."
2. **Thematic strings.** Chains of conceptually related words run through the paragraph.
3. **Announce, then deliver.** New topics and themes are introduced at the *end* of the paragraph's issue, in stress position, and the discussion then develops exactly those.

Williams lists four reasons a paragraph feels "out of focus":
- the issue introduces a theme the discussion drops;
- the discussion develops themes the issue never announced;
- the theme is expressed in terms "so varied that readers cannot connect them" (in one of his examples, thirteen different terms stand for two ideas);
- the themes are buried mid-sentence.

His summary: "you may have made a promise but didn't deliver, or you may have delivered on promises you didn't make."

**Inevitable vs. assembled [SYN]:**
- An assembled paragraph is a set of true sentences about the same subject.
- An inevitable paragraph is a sequence. Each sentence takes up something the previous one put in play, in its topic position, and advances it, in its stress position. The last sentence either closes the thought or opens the need the next paragraph meets.

**Knowledge caveat** (Williams): "readers familiar with the history of that period would not have needed those words." Expert readers supply their own connections. How much explicit stringing you need depends on the audience (§11.4).

> **Weak**
> Remote work has changed offices. Many companies now have hybrid policies. Commuting costs are a burden for workers. Managers worry about collaboration. Some studies find productivity is unchanged. Real estate markets have been affected.
>
> **Problem**
> Each sentence has a new subject (remote work, companies, commuting costs, managers, studies, real estate), so there is no topic string. Nothing links one sentence to the next. You could reorder them freely, which is how you can tell the paragraph was assembled.
>
> **Improved**
> What managers fear about remote work is losing collaboration, and the fear is reasonable if collaboration means the unplanned hallway conversation. That kind of collaboration is exactly what a hybrid schedule fails to restore. If each person comes in on different days, the hallway is empty on any given day. So the typical hybrid policy pays the full price of the office, the commute, while buying back almost none of the benefit it was designed to recover.
>
> **Why it works**
> The topic string holds (the managers' fear, that collaboration, the hybrid schedule, the hybrid policy). Each sentence begins with what the last one ended on and pushes it one step further. The final sentence both closes the reasoning and raises an obvious next question (then what *would* restore it?), so the following paragraph is already needed.

### 5.3 Length, emphasis, endings

- **Length:** no empirical source says what length is ideal. Clark [ADV]: "Vary the lengths of paragraphs… to match your intent." A paragraph ends when its unit of thought ends. A very short paragraph gives its content emphasis, and that emphasis runs out quickly with repeated use.
- **Paragraph endings** are stress positions writ large [SYN from Gopen & Swan]. End a paragraph on the thing that matters most or the thing the next paragraph picks up. Don't end on a qualification or a citation.
- **Links between paragraphs:** Harvey's **stitching** means repeating a key term or phrase from the last paragraph at the point of transition. That kind of link comes from the content. Empty verbal bridges ("Another important point is…", "The preceding paragraph demonstrated…") are what Pinker and Klinkenborg reject. Klinkenborg's alternative: "Make a second sentence that arises from the first sentence."

## 6. Sentences

### 6.1 Reader expectations: topic position, stress position, subject-verb proximity

Gopen & Swan (1990) [RP; they appeal to cognitive psychology but show no experiments, only worked revisions]:

- **Subject-verb separation.** "Readers expect a grammatical subject to be followed immediately by the verb. Anything of length that intervenes… is read as an interruption, and therefore as something of lesser importance."
- **Stress position.** "The stress position coincides with the moment of syntactic closure." The end of the sentence is where readers put emphasis. A colon or semicolon creates a secondary stress position.
- **Topic position.** The beginning of the sentence supplies "linkage (looking backward) and context (looking forward)" and holds "the person, thing or concept whose story it is."
- **Old before new.** Putting new information in the topic position and old information at the end is "the No. 1 problem in American professional writing today." It happens because writers "rush to record" what's new to them.
- **Length.** "A sentence is too long when it has more viable candidates for stress positions than there are stress positions available." They have seen "10-word sentences that are virtually impenetrable and… 100-word sentences that flow effortlessly."
- **Their own disclaimer:** "None of these reader-expectation principles should be considered 'rules'… Our best stylists turn out to be our most skillful violators."

**The empirical anchors:**
- Haviland & Clark (1974) [EMP, abstract]: sentences are understood faster when their given information has a direct antecedent in the preceding context.
- Gibson's dependency locality theory [EMP, strong for extreme cases, modest for ordinary prose; via Temperley & Gildea's review]: the difficulty of processing a sentence rises with the distance between words that depend on each other. Center-embedding and object-relative clauses are the extreme cases. In newspaper eye-tracking the effect was "significant though fairly small." Language corpora across 37 languages show dependencies kept shorter than chance.

**Principle [SYN]:** long sentences are fine if each part attaches close to what it modifies and the sentence carries one line of thought. What costs the reader is *distance*: subjects waiting for verbs, and modifiers stacked before their heads.

### 6.2 Characters as subjects, actions as verbs; nominalization

Williams [RP, excerpt]: "Make Main Characters Subjects," "Make Important Actions Verbs." When nominalizations become subjects, you get the hallmark of "turgid academic and professional writing." Pinker's term is "zombie nouns," which "embalm" a "perfectly spry verb" into a "lifeless noun." Metaconcepts such as *level, strategy, issue, approach, framework* become empty containers [RP].

**When a nominalization is right** (Williams):
1. It refers back to the previous sentence ("These arguments…"), which is the topic-position linkage from §6.1.
2. It replaces "the fact that."
3. It names what a verb acts on ("her request").
4. The concept is so familiar it acts as a character ("Taxation without representation…").

**Diagnostic** (Williams): skip any short introductory phrase and underline the first seven or eight words of each sentence. Flag it if the subject is an abstract noun, or if the verb hasn't arrived yet.

> **Weak**
> The implementation of the policy resulted in a reduction in the utilization of emergency services by residents.
>
> **Problem**
> The real characters (the city, residents) and the real actions (implement, reduce, use) are buried in nouns. The grammatical subject, "the implementation," is an abstraction, and the sentence doesn't say who did what.
>
> **Improved**
> After the city put the policy in place, residents used emergency rooms less.
>
> **Why it works**
> The characters are the subjects and the actions are the verbs. It is half the length and loses nothing. It also shows a gap the original hid: the improved version says "after," not "because." The nominalized sentence ("resulted in") asserted causation without anyone noticing.

That last point generalizes. Gopen & Swan report that structural revision "revealed existing conceptual gaps" [RP]. Clarifying a sentence often shows that the thought behind it was unfinished.

### 6.3 Passive voice: when it helps and when it hurts

Orwell's rule iv ("Never use the passive where you can use the active") and Strunk & White's advice against the passive are the textbook position. The linguists take it apart:

- Orwell's essay uses passives in about 20% of transitive verbs, against roughly 13% in typical prose (Pullum, citing MWDEU). Crystal: "rules 2 to 5 are hugely problematic. In fact they are unworkable." [RP]
- Three of Strunk & White's four "passive" examples aren't passives (Pullum 2009).
- **Pullum's mechanism** [RP, full text]. The passive is an *information-structure tool*. It keeps the discourse topic as the subject and moves new or heavy material to the end. "Have you heard the news about YouTube? It was bought by Google" is right. The active would put the new information (Google) first and break the old-before-new order.
- "The passive construction has absolutely nothing to do with the notion of being vague about agency" (Language Log). Agentless actives can be just as vague.

**When the passive helps:**
- the actor is unknown, obvious, or unimportant;
- the thing acted on is the topic ("Kennedy was assassinated…" in a piece about Kennedy);
- it moves a long or new element to the stress position.

Clark: "Use passive verbs to showcase the 'victim' of action." [ADV]

**When the passive hurts:**
- it **hides responsibility** where responsibility is the point ("mistakes were made");
- it breaks old-before-new, putting a long new agent in a by-phrase after a subject that's also new.

The failure is rhetorical or ethical, not grammatical.

> **Weak (active, but wrong)**
> The paper's central finding is that cortisol levels predict relapse. A team at the university's addiction clinic, working with records from four hundred patients over six years, measured those levels.
>
> **Improved (passive, right)**
> The paper's central finding is that cortisol levels predict relapse. Those levels were measured over six years in four hundred patients at the university's addiction clinic.
>
> **Why it works**
> "Those levels" is old information, and the passive puts it in topic position, linking straight back to the previous sentence. The new detail about scale and setting lands in stress position. The active version makes the reader carry a long new subject before learning how it connects.

And the reverse:

> **Weak**
> Errors were made in the handling of the complaints, and some families were not informed.
>
> **Improved**
> The agency lost complaints and failed to tell families.
>
> **Why it works**
> Here the question of who is responsible is the whole point, and the passive hid the answer.

### 6.4 Emphasis, rhythm, variation, parallelism, repetition

- **Emphasis** follows position (§6.1). Clark: "Place strong words at the beginning and at the end." [ADV] To emphasize something, move it to the end of the sentence. Adding "very," "crucially," or italics doesn't work.
- **Rhythm.** Clark: "Set the pace with sentence length," and "Establish a pattern, then give it a twist." Klinkenborg: "Read aloud. The ear is smarter than the eye." [ADV] There is no empirical research on essay rhythm. The consistent professional advice is to *vary* sentence length for a purpose, not on a schedule. A mechanical long-short-long alternation is its own monotony.
- **Length.** Readability formulas set word limits. Gopen & Swan reject them and count candidates for emphasis instead. Klinkenborg: "Strong, lengthy sentences are really just strong, short sentences joined in various ways." He also warns that long sentences can collapse. A long sentence works when it has one spine and its clauses branch off after the main verb. Clark: "Make meaning early, then let weaker elements branch to the right."
- **Parallelism** makes a comparison visible: "To live without self-respect is to lie awake some night…" (Didion). Williams: arrange coordinated elements "from shorter to longer." Parallelism used by reflex turns into the triplet tic.
- **Repetition.** *Lexical* repetition of a key term is good. It holds the thematic string together, and Harvey says keyterms should "appear throughout (not be abandoned half-way)." "Elegant variation" (thirteen words for two ideas) makes the reader wonder whether you mean different things. *Conceptual* repetition, the same point made again in new words, is the failure. Sommers found that students noticed only lexical repetition, never conceptual [EMP].
- **Concrete vs. abstract.** See §11.3. Clark: "Climb up and down the ladder of abstraction." Pinker: "An explanation without an example is little better than no explanation at all."

### 6.5 On rules in general

Pullum on "Omit needless words": "The students who know which words are needless don't need the instruction." Orwell's own best advice isn't his six rules. It is his four questions: "What am I trying to say? What words will express it? What image or idiom will make it clearer? Is this image fresh enough to have an effect?" His other advice was "let the meaning choose the word, and not the other way about." [RP] Crystal: the rules "work well enough in a negative way — as a critique of obscure writing… but they are no guide to a good style."

**Synthesis [SYN]:** sentence-level advice works best as a diagnostic for finding sentences that are likely to trouble readers. It works badly as a set of rules for drafting. Williams frames his principles that way explicitly.

## 7. Openings

### 7.1 What an opening has to do

An opening has two jobs. It has to establish **motive** (why this needs saying), and it has to make a **promise** about what follows. McPhee: "The lead — like the title — should be a flashlight that shines down into the story. A lead is a promise." [ADV, secondary]

**Harvey's account [RP] explains why openings feel compelling or manipulative.** A scene, anecdote, question, or surprising fact is only a *vehicle*. It works when it carries a motive, meaning it sets up the "But." It feels manipulative when the vehicle has no motive: a dramatic hook that the piece never cashes. It feels slow when the motive takes too long to appear.

### 7.2 The techniques compared

| Opening | Works when | Fails when |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct claim** | The reader already cares about the subject; commentary; the claim is surprising on its face | The claim needs context to be surprising, and so lands as merely assertive |
| **Shared assumption, then "But"** (Orwell, Graham) | You're correcting a common view | Nobody actually holds the view (straw motive) |
| **Scene / anecdote** (Baldwin, Didion) | The case *embodies* the problem and will be reinterpreted later | It's decoration; the piece that follows is a different kind of thing (McPhee's broken promise); it goes on too long before the point |
| **Question** | It's a real question the reader can see they can't yet answer (Loewenstein's information gap) | It's rhetorical, or answered in the next sentence, or too broad to have an answer |
| **Paradox / tension** | The tension is real and the piece resolves or reframes it | It's a verbal trick |
| **Surprising fact** | The fact is the motive (it violates an expectation the piece explains) | It's trivia unrelated to the thesis (a "seductive detail," §11.5) |
| **Problem** | Analysis and policy writing | The problem is stated abstractly ("challenges exist") |
| **Historical example** | The past case sheds light on the present one structurally | "Since the dawn of time…" |

### 7.3 Bad openings and why they fail

From UNC, Pinker, Graham, and the close readings [CONV/SYN]:

- **The placeholder** ("Throughout history, people have debated…"). There's no motive, and it says nothing the reader didn't know.
- **The restated prompt** or the announced subject ("This essay will explore…"). That's metadiscourse. Pinker's fix is to open with the question itself.
- **The dictionary definition.** "Anyone can look a word up in the dictionary." It has authority without motive.
- **The sweeping generalization / "dawn of man."** It denies nothing and promises everything.
- **The hook that betrays the piece.** A dramatic anecdote followed by a dry policy argument breaks the promise.
- **The slow wind-up.** Three paragraphs of context before the reader learns why they're reading.

None of the four exemplary essays in §14 opens with a definition, a historical sweep, or an announcement of structure. All four put something concrete, or a shared assumption, in front of the reader and complicate it within a sentence or two.

UNC advises **writing the introduction last** [CONV]. That fits Sommers's finding that the only idea-level revision students made was trying alternate introductions (§12). The opening is often where a writer discovers what the piece argues.

> **Weak**
> In today's world, technology plays an increasingly important role in our lives. Few issues are more debated than the role of smartphones in schools. This essay will examine both sides of the debate.
>
> **Problem**
> The placeholder, the importance claim, and the metadiscourse, in that order. Three sentences in, the reader knows the topic and nothing else. There's no motive, and "both sides" promises no position.
>
> **Improved**
> Schools that ban phones usually justify it as protecting attention. The teachers who report the biggest change, though, rarely mention attention. What they notice is lunch, which has become loud again.
>
> **Why it works**
> The standard justification comes first, then the "But." The concrete detail (lunch, loud) carries the motive and promises the piece's real subject: phones and social life, not phones and focus. It doesn't announce anything.

## 8. Conclusions

### 8.1 Why restating the thesis fails

Graham traces the restating conclusion to school: it seemed to "restate what we said in the first paragraph, but in different enough words that no one could tell. Why bother?" It's a leftover from the jury summation [ADV]. UNC calls this the "That's My Story and I'm Sticking to It" conclusion. It "does not push the ideas forward." [CONV]

The mechanism [SYN]: a conclusion that only restates does no new intellectual work, so the reader learns at the end that the end has nothing in it. Worse, a restatement written in "different enough words" invites the reader to wonder whether the new wording means something new.

### 8.2 What conclusions can do

Harvey's late "reflecting" moves [RP]:
- **Implication:** "so what?… what might it lead to if I'm right?"
- **Explanation:** why might this be so?
- **Qualification:** "what you're not saying."
- **A newly emerging concern.**

These are "common moves of conclusion." The "why" section in Abrams's structure belongs here too.

Other functions:
- **Reframe.** Show the problem was a different kind of problem.
- **Complicate.** Name what the argument leaves open.
- **Return with a change.** Bring back an image from the opening, reinterpreted. Baldwin's Chartres cathedral comes back to mean something it couldn't mean at first.
- **Final conceptual shift.** Didion: "one runs away to find oneself, and finds no one at home."

DeSilva's three functions of an ending, via Scanlan [CONV]: tell the reader the story is over, nail the central point in memory, and resonate.

**Returning to the opening works when the return reinterprets it and fails when it's decoration** [SYN, from the UNC vs. Scanlan disagreement]. The default quote ending is "overused to the point of cliché" (Scanlan).

**Know the ending early.** McPhee, Fuson, and Clark ("Write toward an ending") all recommend it [ADV]. This is compatible with discovery-first drafting. You may not know the ending on the first draft, but you should know it before the final one.

**What conclusions must not do** (UNC): introduce the thesis for the first time at the end, introduce new *evidence*, or make sentimental appeals out of keeping with the rest of the piece. The zoom-out to "what this says about us all" is the grandiose version of the broadening move. It becomes grandiose when the broadening is asserted instead of earned.

> **Weak**
> In conclusion, as this essay has shown, abolishing library fines has many benefits for communities. Libraries should consider this important policy.
>
> **Improved**
> If the fines were keeping people out of the building rather than keeping books on the shelves, then the question they answered was never really about late returns. It was about who a public room is for. That question doesn't go away when the fines do. It only becomes easier to see.
>
> **Why it works**
> It carries out Harvey's implication move. The argument's result reframes the debate and points to what remains open. Nothing is restated, and the reader leaves with a shift, not a summary.

## 9. Commentary and opinion

### 9.1 Having an opinion vs. having something worth publishing about it

The editors converge on a few requirements [ADV/CONV]:

- **Novelty × importance.** Trish Hall (former NYT op-ed editor): readers want to "learn something they don't already know, and they want to feel like it matters." This is Graham's test again.
- **A position.** Neel Patel (NYT): pieces "cannot just bear witness to some experience… They must stake out a position that they work to defend." Hall: "you have to have some kind of conclusion."
- **Topic vs. theme.** Seglin's op-ed guide (Harvard Kennedy School) separates the **topic** ("the person, place, issue, incident, or thing") from the **theme** ("another level of meaning to the topic… What's your point? Why is your point important?"). An opinion is a stance on a topic. Something worth publishing has a theme.
- **Standing and timing.** Why you, and why now (Patel).

**Synthesis [SYN]:** commentary adds analytical value when it does something the news coverage didn't. Commentary can:
- supply a mechanism (why this happened);
- supply a comparison (what this is like, and where the likeness breaks);
- reveal an assumption (what everyone is taking for granted);
- identify a consequence (what follows that nobody has noticed);
- bring a distinction (two things being treated as one).

An opinion that only rates an event (good, bad, outrageous) adds nothing the reader couldn't supply themselves.

### 9.2 Fact vs. interpretation

C. P. Scott (1921): "Comment is free, but facts are sacred." [CONV] The mechanism [SYN from Kovach & Rosenstiel]: keep the evidence visibly separate from the inference. Then a reader can accept your facts while rejecting your interpretation, and that makes the interpretation *contestable*, which makes it credible. Commentary that fuses fact and judgment ("the disastrous decision to…") asks the reader to take both on trust at once.

### 9.3 Uncertainty and confidence

The research on hedging [EMP, moderate, with moderators]:

- **Hedging limitations increases trust.** Jensen (2008, N = 601) found scientists and journalists were seen as more trustworthy when cancer news reported study limitations, especially when the scientists themselves voiced them. The finding concerns trustworthiness, not perceived expertise.
- **Quantified uncertainty costs little.** van der Bles et al. (2020, five experiments, n = 5,780) found only a small drop in trust, mostly for *verbal* uncertainty. Numeric ranges had little effect.
- **Type of uncertainty matters.** Gustafson & Rice (2020, review of 48 experiments) found that technical uncertainty (ranges, probabilities) had "only positive or null effects." Consensus uncertainty (experts disagreeing) was "never found to have positive effects."
- **Location of the hedge matters.** Durik et al. (2008, secondary only): hedges on *data* statements hurt. Colloquial hedges on interpretations hurt too.
- **Prior beliefs moderate all of it.** Dries et al. (2025): how trustworthy uncertainty seems depends on whether the evidence fits the reader's priors.

**Synthesis: calibrated confidence [SYN].**
- State flatly what you know. Graham: "Something you're sure of, you can state baldly with no qualification at all."
- Qualify precisely what you don't know. Give numbers where you have them, and name the specific limitation.
- Hedge the *inference*, not the *fact*.
- Qualification is "not just a sort of tax you have to pay" (Graham). "'I think x' is a weaker statement than simply 'x'… Which is exactly why you need 'I think.'"
- Pinker's distinction: reflexive hedges ("wads of fluff," "I would argue") are bad. A qualification that spells out "the circumstances in which it does not hold" is good.
- Galef: *epistemic* confidence (how sure you are) is separate from *social* confidence (how self-assured you sound). A writer can be assured in manner and calibrated in substance [ADV, secondary].

**The commentator's structural temptation** [EMP]. Tetlock's forecasting research (Mellers et al., abstract) found that "hedgehogs" with one big idea predicted worse than "foxes." Media prominence plausibly rewards confident hedgehogs (secondary). The market rewards confidence regardless of calibration, so calibration has to be a deliberate choice.

> **Weak (overqualified)**
> It could perhaps be argued that the policy may, in some cases, have possibly contributed to some reduction in crime.
>
> **Weak (underqualified)**
> The policy slashed crime.
>
> **Improved**
> Burglaries fell by about a third in the two years after the policy began. The policy plausibly deserves some of the credit. How much is unclear, because burglaries were also falling in neighboring cities that did nothing.
>
> **Why it works**
> The fact is stated flatly. The causal inference is hedged once, at the right place, and the hedge has content: a specific alternative explanation. The reader knows exactly what's known and what isn't.

### 9.4 Fair criticism and the problem of outrage

- **Outrage is structurally rewarded, so engagement is no evidence of quality** [EMP, moderate]. Rathje et al. (2021, PNAS) found that posts about the political out-group were shared about twice as often, and each out-group term raised the odds of sharing by 67%. The data were correlational.
- **Contempt is the condition under which backlash appears** [EMP, weak to moderate]. Velez & Liu (2024, secondary) found that civil counterarguments produced moderation. Polarization appeared "only when arguments are contentious and vitriolic."
- **Graham's disagreement hierarchy** [ADV], from bottom to top:
  1. name-calling
  2. ad hominem
  3. responding to tone
  4. contradiction
  5. counterargument
  6. refutation
  7. refuting the central point, which "means one has to commit explicitly to what the central point is"

  Graham: "A DH6 response might be unconvincing, but a DH2 or lower response is always unconvincing." He also names a dishonest move: refuting minor points and implying you've refuted the whole.
- **Let the analysis carry the judgment** [SYN]. A harsh verdict that the analysis *earns* is forceful and honest. A merely asserted one reads as performance. (The Economist style guide line "let your analysis show that he is [silly]" was seen only on a quotation site. Verify before quoting.)

### 9.5 Representing opponents accurately

**Rapoport's rules**, via Dennett [ADV]:
1. Re-express the target's position "so clearly, vividly and fairly that your target says: 'Thanks, I wish I'd thought of putting it that way.'"
2. List points of agreement.
3. Mention anything you learned from the target.
4. "Only then are you permitted to say so much as a word of rebuttal or criticism."

The evidence that this makes targets receptive is anecdotal. The underlying move, refuting rather than merely acknowledging (§2.3), has empirical support.

**Disagreement: steelmanning vs. the Ideological Turing Test** [ADV]. Bensinger argues that steelmanning replaces a person's view with a better one and so misrepresents them. He prefers passing an ITT, meaning you describe their view so well that they'd endorse your description. Zack M. Davis replies that improving arguments is what truth-seekers do anyway.

**Resolution [SYN]:**
- When criticizing a **named person or text**, represent what they actually said at ITT standard. Accuracy is an ethical duty there.
- When assessing an **idea**, steelman it, and label it as your reconstruction: "the strongest version of this view, which X may not hold, is…"

**The consistency check** (Scott Alexander) [ADV]: apply the same standard of evidence to views you like and views you don't.

### 9.6 Persuasion expectations

Coppock's *Persuasion in Parallel* (23 experiments; draft chapter, full text) [EMP, moderate]: persuasive effects are "small, positive, and durable for everyone." That is about five percentage points, or a tenth of a standard deviation, for an op-ed. Opponents and proponents move in the same direction by similar amounts. Guess & Coppock (2020): "no evidence of backlash."

**Implication [SYN]:** don't write as if the goal were a conversion. A good piece moves a committed opposing reader a little, and it keeps its credibility with them so the next piece can move them further. O'Keefe's own conclusion is that argument quality matters to persuasion less than one would hope. So the case for rigor rests mainly on **credibility, durability, and honesty**, not on large persuasion gains.

## 10. Voice and style

### 10.1 Style should serve thought

Orwell's diagnosis [RP]: "The great enemy of clear language is insincerity. When there is a gap between one's real and one's declared aims, one turns as it were instinctively to long words and exhausted idioms." Of his own books without a real purpose: he "wrote lifeless books and was betrayed into purple passages, sentences without meaning, decorative adjectives and humbug generally." The mechanism: ornament stands in for having something to say. When a passage is overwritten, look first for what the writer is avoiding saying.

**Pinker's classic style** [RP], from Thomas & Turner: "The writer can see something that the reader has not yet noticed, and he orients the reader's gaze so that she can see it for herself." The writer and reader are equals in conversation, and "prose is a window onto the world." Its opposite is the self-conscious style, whose "chief, if unstated, concern is to escape being convicted of philosophical naïveté." That style produces metadiscourse, apologizing, shudder quotes, compulsive hedging, and zombie nouns.

**The dissent** (Nardo, "Against 'Classic Style'") [ADV]: classic style makes it hard to say "I might be wrong about X." She proposes instead that "authors should write sentences that are maximally informative to the reader." **Resolution [SYN]:** classic style fits claims the writer has actually verified. Explicit uncertainty fits contested, predictive, or evidence-limited claims (§9.3). Graham's "bold, but true" holds both.

### 10.2 Voice is the product of choices, not a decoration applied afterward

The four close readings in §14 have four very different voices: Orwell sardonic, Baldwin grave and periodic, Didion exact and ironic, Graham conversational. Each voice comes from the same place: many small decisions made for this material. Saunders describes the process [ADV]: reading one's own draft "the way a first-time reader might" with an imagined "P/N" meter, asking "Is it better like this? Or like this?" The result is prose "better than I am in 'real life' — funnier, kinder, less full of crap." Nobody plans a voice. It comes out of revision (§12).

### 10.3 The elements

- **Authority** comes from exactness, not from assertion. Didion's hedging is "almost absent," and her authority comes from precise detail (§14). Credibility, in O'Keefe's split, is expertise (in a position to know) plus trustworthiness (inclined to tell the truth). Candor about limits builds the second (Jensen).
- **Confidence and humility** aren't opposites. See calibrated confidence (§9.3) and self-correction as a move (Baldwin: "but this is not quite true").
- **First person** is contested.
  - Zinsser urges it (secondary only): writing is "an intimate transaction between two people."
  - Pinker prefers "we" as a shared gaze and objects to narrating the researcher's activity.
  - Didion calls saying "I" "an aggressive, even a hostile act."
  - NYT's Patel: first person "needs to do something in the piece."
  - **Resolution [SYN]:** use "I" when the writer's experience or judgment is itself evidence, or when a claim is honestly personal. Avoid it when it only narrates the writing ("I will argue").
  - The style profile governs this locally.
- **Humor** isn't treated systematically in the research. Sword's descriptive corpus of 1,000 academic articles (secondary) found humor among the traits of "stylish" academic writers. That's a correlation, not evidence that humor causes quality. The local rules live in the style profile.
- **Metaphor and analogy** are decoration unless they carry logic. Orwell's drink analogy ("A man may take to drink because he feels himself to be a failure, and then fail all the more completely because he drinks") *is* his causal argument, that a cause and its effect can reinforce each other. His test: "Is this image fresh enough to have an effect?" A forced metaphor is one whose second meaning does no work. Test it like an argument from analogy (§2.4).
- **Rhetorical questions** work when they open a real information gap that the text then pursues. They fail when they're answered in the next sentence, which is a fake gap, or stacked, which is a performance of inquiry.
- **Irony and parentheticals** (Didion's "(preferably a cross between Humphrey Bogart in Casablanca and one of the Murchisons in a proxy fight)") work when the aside adds a second perspective. They fail when they're verbal tics.

### 10.4 Style can't rescue a weak thought

Oppenheimer (2006) [EMP, moderate for general fluency effects; weak to moderate for this specific claim]: needlessly complex vocabulary *lowered* readers' ratings of the author's intelligence. 86% of students admitted inflating their language to sound smart. The author's own caveat: "When a long word is actually the best word for the occasion, it very well may be that using it will lead to positive appraisals." Precise technical words weren't tested. Only needless inflation was.

**Implication [SYN]:** sophistication that doesn't come from the thought doesn't just fail. It costs credibility.

## 11. Reader psychology

The research here gives a small number of well-supported principles and a larger number of contested ones. The table rates them.

| Principle | Evidence | Strength | What it means for writing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Explicit links help readers who lack background | McNamara et al. 1996; Britton & Gülgöz 1991; Kintsch 1994 | Strong | For general readers, make each sentence's connection to the last explicit through referential overlap, connectives, and stated causes. **The best-supported principle in this section.** |
| Gaps help experts | McNamara 1996 reverse-cohesion effect | Weak / contested | Narrow effect; didn't appear for persuasive texts (Kamalski 2008); skilled experts preferred cohesion (O'Reilly & McNamara 2007). Don't leave gaps "for engagement." |
| Needless complexity lowers judged intelligence | Oppenheimer 2006 | Moderate | Don't inflate. |
| Fluency makes claims feel truer | Alter & Oppenheimer 2009; Dechêne et al. 2010 | Moderate | Clarity persuades independently of accuracy. That's an ethical obligation as well as a tool. |
| Deliberate disfluency deepens thinking | Alter 2007 → Meyer et al. 2015 (16 replications, null) | **Failed to replicate** | Never make prose harder on purpose. |
| Concreteness aids recall and interest | Sadoski et al.; >2:1 recall advantage | Moderate–strong (students) | Anchor abstract claims with concrete instances placed right next to them. |
| Interesting-but-irrelevant material hurts | Harp & Mayer 1998; meta-analyses g ≈ −0.16 to −0.33 | Moderate but shrinking; contested (Sadoski) | Vivid material that isn't the point costs the point. If you keep an aside, put it after the core idea is established. |
| Curiosity gaps drive attention and memory | Loewenstein 1994; Kang 2009; Gruber 2014 | Moderate (lab, trivia) | Open real questions; resolve them with something non-obvious; teasers with trivial answers "disappoint when satisfied." |
| Narrative transportation shifts beliefs, reduces counterarguing | Green & Brock 2000; van Laer 2014; Braddock & Dillard 2016 (r ≈ .17–.19) | Moderate | Cases persuade, and they reduce critical thought, which cuts both ways ethically. |
| Refutation corrects misconceptions | Schroeder & Kucera 2022 (g = 0.41; adults ~0.33); Ecker et al. 2022 | Moderate–strong | State the misconception once, say clearly it's wrong, explain *why*, and supply a better causal account. |
| Correcting a myth backfires | Wood & Porter 2019 (10,100 subjects, none); Swire-Thompson 2020 | **Mostly rejected** | Don't avoid naming the opposing view out of fear of spreading it. |
| Biased evaluation of arguments | Taber & Lodge 2006 | Strong | Readers rate congenial arguments as stronger. Expect it and don't take agreement as confirmation. |
| Balanced arguments polarize | Guess & Coppock 2020; Coppock | Mostly not found | Small same-direction updating is the norm. |
| Signals improve memory of what they signal | Lorch 1989; Schneider 2018 | Moderate–strong | Signal what matters, not everything. |
| Load is relative to expertise | Kalyuga et al. 2003 (expertise reversal) | Strong (instruction) | Decide who the reader is before deciding how much to spell out. |
| Long dependencies cost processing | Gibson; Temperley & Gildea | Strong for extremes, small in ordinary prose | Keep subjects near verbs; avoid center-embedding. |
| Multiple aligned examples beat one | Atkinson et al. 2000 | Moderate | Give more than one example and make explicit what they share. Example-first vs. claim-first is **unsettled**. |

### 11.1 The tensions to hold

- **Engagement vs. focus.** Curiosity, narrative, and concreteness all raise engagement. Seductive details show that engagement bought with irrelevant material costs comprehension. The dividing line is **relevance to the main point** [SYN]. Concreteness that *is* the point helps, and vividness *next to* the point hurts.
- **Explicit vs. productive difficulty.** Cohesion helps novices. Gaps help only a narrow group of experts. Surface disfluency helps nobody.
- **Fluency's double edge.** The same ease that makes a true argument land makes a false one feel true. A writer who takes fluency seriously owes the reader correspondingly more accuracy.

### 11.2 The limits, again

These findings mostly come from students reading short passages and tested immediately. Transfer to long-form commentary read voluntarily by adults is untested almost everywhere. Use the mechanisms. Don't quote the effect sizes as if they described essays.

## 12. Revision

### 12.1 What experienced writers do differently

Sommers (1980) [EMP, small qualitative study: 20 students, 20 experienced writers; full text]:

- **Students** saw revision as "a rewording activity," a "thesaurus philosophy of writing." They noticed lexical repetition but "not conceptual repetition." They sensed larger problems but had no tools for them: "I knew something larger was wrong, but I didn't think it would help to move words around." They stopped revising once they had broken no rules. The only idea-level change they made was trying alternate introductions.
- **Experienced writers** made "finding the form or shape of their argument" their primary aim. Their second concern was an imagined reader. Revision was recursive: "each addition or deletion is a reordering of the whole." They deliberately put off word-level concerns until later passes.

The practitioners converge [SYN]:
- Murray's "When a draft is completed, the job of writing can begin" (secondary).
- Clark's "Limit self-criticism in early drafts. Turn it loose during revision."
- Graham going "back seven paragraphs and start over in another direction."
- Pinker's "another draft (or two or three or four)."

**Revise from the top down: shape and argument first, words last.** Polishing sentences that a structural revision will delete is wasted work.

### 12.2 The passes

Each pass asks one kind of question. Mixing them is how word-level fussing crowds out structural thinking.

1. **Ideas.** Is there a real motive (§1.2)? Does the thesis pass the so-what and Davis tests (§1.5)? Did I learn something writing it? *Look for:* the point in the last paragraph that should be the first (Williams); a thesis that has quietly shifted during drafting.
2. **Argument.** State the warrants. Run the critical questions (§2.4). Does every objection raised get an answer? Would I accept this reasoning for a conclusion I dislike? *Look for:* non-refutational concessions, hidden causal claims, a strongest objection that's missing.
3. **Evidence.** Is every piece of evidence inside a claim → evidence → interpretation → implication chain? Is anything asserted beyond what I know? Is any example unrepresentative? Is there an obvious counterexample I left out? *Look for:* evidence dumps, lone anecdotes carrying general claims.
4. **Structure.** Make a **reverse outline** (Caltech): one short phrase for each paragraph's function. "If you find it difficult to express the main idea in a short phrase, that may be an indicator that the paragraph needs to undergo revision." Then run the **shuffle test** (§4.1), the **first-sentences read-through** (does the sequence progress or list?), and the **walk-through test** (does the order follow the sources or the argument?).
5. **Paragraphs.** Topic strings, and the promise audit: does each paragraph develop exactly the themes its opening announced (§5.2)? Does each end on what matters or on what the next picks up?
6. **Sentences.** Check the first seven or eight words (Williams), the subject-verb gap, the stress position (Gopen & Swan), and nominalizations. Check each passive for whether it serves information flow or hides an agent (Pullum).
7. **Word choice.** Are key terms consistent (no elegant variation)? Replace metaconcepts and zombie nouns. Apply Orwell's four questions to every image. Check the style profile's Avoid list.
8. **Rhythm.** Read aloud (Klinkenborg, Clark). Listen for monotony, collapsing long sentences, and a mechanical alternation of long and short.
9. **Fact-check.** Check every factual claim, quotation, number, and attribution against its source. In Spitball, also check every claim against the raw thoughts and the context-research list (§15).
10. **Cut.** Clark: "Cut big, then small." McPhee's "greening" exercise: cut lines "in such a manner that no one would notice that anything has been removed." Graham: "The boring bits can usually be fixed by cutting."

### 12.3 Deciding what to do with a problem

Graham, Pinker, and Sommers converge on a diagnostic mapping [SYN]:

| Symptom (from a reader or your own cold reread) | Usual cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Boring** | Material the argument doesn't need; conceptual repetition | **Cut** |
| **Unconvincing** | The claim is wrong or too broad | **Rethink the claim**, not the rhetoric (Graham) |
| **Unclear** | Curse of knowledge: missing steps, no example | **Expand**: add the step, the example, the stated link |
| **Out of order** | Walk-through structure; point discovered late | **Move** |
| **Unsupported** | A claim that needs evidence you don't have | **Research**, narrow the claim, or mark a gap |
| **Flat, but correct** | Sentences that work but don't land | **Rewrite** at the sentence level, last |

**The curse of knowledge can't be fixed by introspection.** Pinker: beating it "requires showing a draft to a sample of real readers," or rereading after enough time that the draft is "no longer familiar." [RP, grounded in EMP constructs]

## 13. Failure modes

Each entry gives the symptom, the underlying cause, why it hurts the writing, and the fix.

**Idea-level**

- **No meaningful thesis.**
  - Symptom: a piece "about" a topic.
  - Cause: the writer stopped at the topic or question rung (§1.1).
  - Why it hurts: the reader can't tell what to take away, and nothing organizes the material.
  - Fix: the three-step formula; find the "But."
- **Obvious thesis.**
  - Symptom: every reader already agrees.
  - Cause: no motive; a thesis that affirms the reader's assumptions (Davis).
  - Why it hurts: there's nothing to learn.
  - Fix: find the assumption a smart reader actually holds and deny it, or drop the piece.
- **Thesis too broad.**
  - Symptom: it's true of almost anything, or "has both positive and negative effects."
  - Cause: the writer is protecting correctness by being vague (Graham).
  - Why it hurts: it can't be argued, so it can't be interesting.
  - Fix: narrow to the most precise version you can still defend.
- **Straw motive.**
  - Symptom: "Many people think X" where nobody does.
  - Cause: the thesis needed something to correct.
  - Why it hurts: informed readers stop trusting the writer.
  - Fix: find the real misapprehension (Harvey: one "an intelligent reader would plausibly have"), or change the thesis.

**Argument-level**

- **Argument by assertion.**
  - Symptom: claims follow claims with no grounds or warrants.
  - Cause: the writer finds the claim obvious (curse of knowledge) or is performing confidence.
  - Why it hurts: skeptical readers have nothing to evaluate, and agreeing readers learn nothing.
  - Fix: for each contested claim, supply the grounds and state the warrant.
- **Unsupported causal claim.**
  - Symptom: "led to," "resulted in," "drove," with only sequence as evidence.
  - Cause: nominalizations and inflated verbs hide the leap (§6.2).
  - Why it hurts: it's the most common way a correct observation becomes a false claim.
  - Fix: name the mechanism and the alternatives, or downgrade to "followed" or "is associated with."
- **Non-refutational concession.**
  - Symptom: "Of course, some will object… But…"
  - Cause: counterargument treated as a ritual.
  - Why it hurts: it's worse than silence (O'Keefe).
  - Fix: answer the objection or cut it.
- **Straw man / weak-objection rebuttal.**
  - Symptom: the opposing view as its opponents would state it.
  - Cause: myside bias (Mercier & Sperber).
  - Why it hurts: it costs trust and reasonableness, with no persuasive gain (Younis).
  - Fix: Rapoport's first rule, or the ITT.
- **False dichotomy.**
  - Symptom: "Either we X or we Y."
  - Cause: the key terms create the binary (Harvey).
  - Why it hurts: it hides the options the reader may prefer.
  - Fix: ask what else is possible.
- **Weak analogy.**
  - Symptom: a comparison that feels apt but carries no logic.
  - Cause: surface similarity mistaken for structural similarity.
  - Why it hurts: the conclusion rests on a likeness that isn't relevant.
  - Fix: Bartha's test. Is the similarity relevant to *this* conclusion, and is there a critical disanalogy?
- **Cherry-picking.**
  - Symptom: every piece of evidence points one way.
  - Cause: selection after the conclusion was fixed.
  - Why it hurts: informed readers know the counterevidence exists.
  - Fix: Harvey's "no obvious pieces of evidence overlooked." Address the best counterexample.

**Evidence-level**

- **Evidence dumping.**
  - Symptom: paragraphs of facts, quotes, numbers.
  - Cause: the writer confuses having evidence with using it.
  - Why it hurts: the reader has to do the interpretation, or doesn't.
  - Fix: the claim → evidence → interpretation → implication chain; at least as much analysis as evidence (UNC).
- **Summary instead of analysis.**
  - Symptom: the piece restates its sources in order.
  - Cause: a walk-through structure (Abrams).
  - Why it hurts: it adds nothing to the sources.
  - Fix: organize by the argument; each source enters to do a job.
- **The lone anecdote.**
  - Symptom: one vivid case carrying a general claim.
  - Cause: narrative is persuasive, even to the writer.
  - Why it hurts: it's a generalization from n = 1.
  - Fix: say why it's representative, or pair it with evidence that is.

**Structure-level**

- **Mechanical structure.**
  - Symptom: every section has the same shape; intro previews, sections summarize, conclusion restates.
  - Cause: a template applied instead of an argument followed.
  - Why it hurts: the reader can predict everything, so nothing lands.
  - Fix: build the structure from the reader's questions (§4.1).
- **List structure.**
  - Symptom: paragraphs could be shuffled.
  - Cause: no development, only accumulation (Harvey's Macbeth).
  - Why it hurts: the argument never advances.
  - Fix: find the complication and let each unit create the next unit's need.
- **Repetition disguised as emphasis.**
  - Symptom: the thesis restated at the end of every section.
  - Cause: conceptual repetition that the writer can't see (Sommers).
  - Why it hurts: it reads as padding, and it wastes the stress positions.
  - Fix: reverse outline; cut any paragraph whose function is "restates."
- **Excessive throat-clearing / generic introduction.**
  - Symptom: the actual subject appears in paragraph three.
  - Cause: the writer warming up on the page.
  - Why it hurts: the motive comes too late, and readers leave.
  - Fix: delete until the first sentence does work. Klinkenborg: "Let the first sentence begin the piece. Don't introduce it."
- **Unnecessary conclusion.**
  - Symptom: a summary of what was just read.
  - Cause: school convention (Graham).
  - Why it hurts: it ends on nothing.
  - Fix: Harvey's implication or qualification move, or end on the last real thought.
- **Weak transitions.**
  - Symptom: "Additionally," "Another point," "Furthermore."
  - Cause: the paragraphs aren't logically connected, so the link is asserted with a word.
  - Why it hurts: the bridge advertises that there's no connection.
  - Fix: stitch with the content (a repeated key term, a consequence), or fix the order.

**Sentence- and style-level**

- **Jargon.**
  - Symptom: insider terms that general readers can't unpack.
  - Cause: the curse of knowledge, or group-membership signaling (Billig).
  - Why it hurts: it excludes readers and hides weak claims.
  - Fix: use the plain word, or define the term at first use.
- **Empty sophistication / "smart-sounding" prose.**
  - Symptom: abstract nouns, metaconcepts, long words, little reasoning when paraphrased.
  - Cause: Orwell's "gap between one's real and one's declared aims"; wanting to seem intelligent (Oppenheimer's 86%).
  - Why it hurts: it lowers judged intelligence, and it hides the absence of a thought.
  - Fix: **paraphrase test**. Restate each sentence in plain words. If the paraphrase is trivial or empty, the sentence was too.
- **False profundity.**
  - Symptom: epigrams that sound deep and assert nothing checkable.
  - Cause: an aphoristic *form* without a claim.
  - Why it hurts: attentive readers notice, and the others are misled.
  - Fix: ask what the sentence would mean if false. If you can't say, it means nothing.
- **Overqualification.**
  - Symptom: "may perhaps in some cases possibly."
  - Cause: fear of being wrong, or a writer who hasn't yet formed an identity as an authority (Cayley's point about novices).
  - Why it hurts: the reader can't tell what's claimed.
  - Fix: hedge once, at the inference, with content (§9.3).
- **Underqualification / excessive certainty.**
  - Symptom: universal claims from limited evidence.
  - Cause: rhetorical confidence mistaken for strength.
  - Why it hurts: one counterexample topples it, and it signals miscalibration.
  - Fix: match the qualifier to the grounds.
- **Excessive rhetorical questions.**
  - Symptom: questions answered immediately, or stacked.
  - Cause: a performance of inquiry.
  - Why it hurts: the gaps are fake, so the reader learns to ignore them.
  - Fix: keep only questions the text actually pursues.
- **Forced metaphors.**
  - Symptom: an image whose second meaning does nothing, or mixed metaphors.
  - Cause: decoration sought for its own sake.
  - Why it hurts: it pulls the reader toward a comparison that misleads.
  - Fix: Orwell's freshness question, then Bartha's relevance test.
- **Excessive hedging vs. excessive certainty** are the same failure in two directions: **miscalibration**. The fix for both is to decide what you actually know.

## 14. Close readings: what recurs across very different writers

Four essays were read in full, chosen for range: an argument, a personal-historical essay, a moral essay, and an exploratory essay.

**Orwell, "Politics and the English Language" (1946)**
- *Opening:* a concession, then a "but": "Most people who bother with the matter at all would admit that the English language is in a bad way, but it is generally assumed that we cannot by conscious action do anything about it." It is Harvey's motive in its purest form.
- *Thesis:* stated early and plainly: "the process is reversible." It comes with an analogy that carries the causal logic (the drinker who fails, then drinks, then fails more).
- *Evidence:* five specimen passages, deliberately not the worst ("I could have quoted far worse"). That heads off the charge of cherry-picking.
- *Progression:* specimen → taxonomy of faults → parody → escalation to politics → remedy.
- *Honesty moves:* he admits committing the faults himself, and he limits his scope to non-literary language.
- *Ending:* a modest, practical scope ("one can at least change one's own habits"), and a comic kicker that acts out the thesis.

**Baldwin, "Stranger in the Village" (1953)**
- *Opening:* flat, factual, personal: "From all available evidence no black man had ever set foot in this tiny Swiss village before I came."
- *Thesis:* withheld and built up in stages:
  - the first abstraction arrives at the end of paragraph 5 ("People are trapped in history and history is trapped in them");
  - the pivot comes in paragraph 9 (the village "is the West");
  - the full claim comes only at the end ("This world is white no longer, and it will never be white again").
- *Structure:* a widening spiral from village to self to the West to American history to the world.
- *Motifs:* concrete motifs return transformed. Chartres reappears with a meaning it couldn't have at first.
- *Honesty moves:* self-correction ("but this is not quite true") and repeated fair-minded concessions about the villagers. These make the harder claims harder to dismiss.
- *Sentences:* long, periodic, cumulative, with anaphora and chiasmus.
- *Ending:* it broadens and complicates. American history is "not merely shameful, it is also something of an achievement."

**Didion, "On Self-Respect" (1961)**
- *Opening:* a remembered scene written as confession, with deliberately small stakes. She treats failing to make Phi Beta Kappa as having the tragic stature of Fitzgerald's failure to become president of the Princeton Triangle Club.
- *Thesis:* reached by negation. Self-respect "has nothing to do with the approval of others," is not "a kind of charm against snakes." The positive definition comes only in paragraph 7.
- *Evidence:* literary and cultural examples (Jordan Baker vs. Julian English, an 1846 diary), not data.
- *Counterargument:* dispatched in a single sentence.
- *Style:* parallel infinitive constructions, an extended metaphor (the "interminable home movie"), and ironic parentheticals. Hedging is almost absent, and the authority comes from exactness.
- *Ending:* an aphoristic twist: "one runs away to find oneself, and finds no one at home."

**Graham, "The Age of the Essay" (2004)**
- *Opening:* a shared memory ("Remember the essays you had to write in high school?"), then "Oy." It is the "standard view is wrong" motive in a colloquial register.
- *Thesis:* stated early: "An essay is something you write to try to figure something out."
- *Evidence:* a historical genealogy that explains where the bad convention came from. Once you know its origins, its authority falls apart.
- *Voice:* conversational, first person throughout, jokes.
- *Honesty moves:* he qualifies his own rule ("One can't have quite as little foresight as a river").
- *Ending:* it broadens to the Web, then acts out the thesis: "that's certainly not something I realized when I started writing this."

### What recurs (broadly transferable principles) [SYN, by inspection, not statistics]

1. **The opening puts something concrete or a shared assumption in front of the reader and complicates it almost at once.** None of the four opens with a definition, a historical sweep, or an announcement.
2. **Every abstraction is anchored in a concrete image or case.** The hen-house, Chartres, Jordan Baker, the river.
3. **Credibility is earned by conceding and self-correcting.** All four do it, in four different registers.
4. **Endings broaden, twist, or act out the thesis. None of them summarizes.**
5. **Early material returns transformed.** The return adds meaning. It isn't there for symmetry.
6. **Almost no metadiscourse or reflexive hedging.** Qualifications, where they appear, have content.

### What varies (genre conventions)

- **Thesis placement.** Orwell and Graham state it early, as argument and commentary conventions expect. Baldwin withholds it and builds toward it, as the personal-historical essay does. Didion reaches it by negation at mid-piece.
- **Evidence type.** Specimens (Orwell), lived experience plus history (Baldwin), literary examples (Didion), genealogy plus testimony (Graham).

### What varies (individual style)

- Sentence rhythm: Baldwin's long periodic sentences, Didion's parallel infinitives, Graham's clipped conversation.
- Density of allusion: very high in Didion, almost none in Graham.
- Humor: Orwell's sarcasm, Graham's jokes, Didion's dry irony, Baldwin's near-absence of humor.

**Don't imitate any of these.** The transferable part is the six recurring moves. Everything else belongs to the writer.

A note against a myth: Didion reportedly wrote "On Self-Respect" quickly, to an exact character count, to fill a gap in the magazine (secondary report). Excellence doesn't always come from long revision. It more reliably comes from knowing exactly what you think.

## 15. Applying this inside Spitball

The research above assumes a writer who can go and get evidence. In this vault, the corpus is the epistemic boundary (`CLAUDE.md` §1.2). That changes how several principles apply. **When this section conflicts with anything above, this section and `CLAUDE.md` win.**

- **Evidence means the author's thoughts, plus context research.** "Enough evidence" (§3.1) means enough grounding *in the corpus*. When an argument needs evidence the corpus doesn't have (a statistic, a study, an example the author never gave), the craft instinct to go and get it is exactly what §1.2 forbids. Leave `[gap: needs evidence that X]` and raise it in the post-draft report. Don't fill it from training knowledge.
- **Motive and thesis come from the corpus.** The richest sources of motive in a raw corpus are the author's own tensions, changes of mind, and unresolved questions. That fits Harvey's list, and it fits §1.3 of `CLAUDE.md`, which says to surface tensions rather than resolve them. A proposed thesis is a *proposal* until the author accepts it (§4.1).
- **Counterarguments.** The research says to answer the strongest objection (§2.3). But an objection the author never considered is itself outside intellectual content. Where the corpus contains the objection, or the author's answer, or both, use them. Where it doesn't, **don't invent the objection and a rebuttal on the author's behalf.** Flag it: `[gap: strongest objection is probably X; author's answer needed]`. A raised-but-unanswered objection is worse than silence, so never leave one in a draft without the author's response.
- **Examples** must be the author's, drawn from the corpus. Illustrative examples invented to *clarify* are expression. Examples that *support a claim* are content.
- **Calibration** follows the corpus. Where the author is sure, state it flatly. Where the author wavers, the draft wavers too, precisely, at the point of uncertainty (§9.3 and `CLAUDE.md` §1.3). Don't manufacture confidence the thoughts don't show, and don't hedge what the author asserted.
- **Structure comes from the argument, never from retrieval order.** The walk-through failure (§4.1) is the craft name for what `CLAUDE.md` §1.4 forbids.
- **Revision fact-checking** (§12.2, pass 9) means checking every claim in the draft against the raw thoughts it rests on and against the context-research list in the project files.
- **Style profiles govern expression.** Where a profile sets rules for openings, first person, humor, headers, or its own Avoid list, follow the profile. This file supplies what the profile doesn't specify, and the reasoning standards that no style overrides.

## 16. The Excellent Nonfiction Writing System

Idea → Research → Thesis → Argument → Structure → Drafting → Evidence → Revision → Final Edit. Stages loop back. Drafting often changes the thesis, and when it does, go back rather than forcing the draft to fit.

### Stage 1: Idea

- **Objective:** find something true, important, and not already known to the intended reader.
- **Questions:**
  - Who is the reader, and what do they currently assume?
  - Where is the "But": the tension, puzzle, or misapprehension?
  - Have I thought about this longer than most readers have?
- **Techniques:**
  - Harvey's motive list as a generator.
  - The Davis form ("what seems X is really non-X").
  - Look for the author's own contradictions and changes of mind.
- **Warning signs:**
  - You can state it in four words (a topic).
  - It's true of everything ("positive and negative effects").
  - You can't name who believes the opposite.
- **Quality criteria:** passes the iterated so-what; a representative reader would react "huh," not "obvious," "irrelevant," or "absurd."

### Stage 2: Research

- **Objective:** understand the material well enough to find what's actually there, including what cuts against you.
- **Questions:**
  - What does the material actually say, as opposed to what I expected it to say?
  - What's the strongest evidence against my hunch?
  - Which claims will need support?
- **Techniques:**
  - Gather more than you'll use (McPhee's ten-to-one).
  - Go to primary sources (Kovach & Rosenstiel).
  - Note surprises; they're candidate insights.
  - In Spitball: staged retrieval (`CLAUDE.md` §5), with context research only for situating events (§1.2).
- **Warning signs:**
  - Collecting only confirming material.
  - Research standing in for thinking.
  - Research breadth growing while the question stays vague.
- **Quality criteria:** you can state the best counterevidence; you know which of your claims are well-supported and which aren't.

### Stage 3: Thesis

- **Objective:** a claim that's true, arguable, precise, bounded, and governs the whole piece.
- **Questions:**
  - Is it contestable by a reasonable person?
  - What's the most precise version I can defend?
  - What assumption of the reader's does it deny?
  - What cost does the reader bear if it's true and they don't know it?
- **Techniques:**
  - Booth/Colomb/Williams's three steps.
  - Graham's multiplication test.
  - Draft a working thesis, and expect it to change.
- **Warning signs:**
  - Depends on a dichotomy you haven't argued for (Harvey).
  - Its surprise outruns its evidence (OSC).
  - Correct because vague.
- **Quality criteria:** true × important × novel × strongly stated; limited enough to be argued with the evidence available.

### Stage 4: Argument

- **Objective:** a case a skeptical, fair reader would find sound.
- **Questions:**
  - What are the warrants, and would my audience contest them?
  - What critical questions does each inference face (§2.4)?
  - What's the strongest objection, and what's my answer?
  - Would I accept this reasoning for a conclusion I dislike?
- **Techniques:**
  - Toulmin mapping of the core claims.
  - Walton's critical questions.
  - Rapoport's rules or the ITT for opponents.
  - Myside checks.
  - In Spitball: objections from the corpus, or flagged gaps.
- **Warning signs:**
  - Every piece of evidence points one way.
  - The opponents are caricatures.
  - Causal claims rest only on sequence.
  - A concession with no answer.
- **Quality criteria:** the strongest objection is stated fairly and answered; qualifiers match the grounds; conceding an objection changed the claim instead of being waved away.

### Stage 5: Structure

- **Objective:** an order in which each unit creates the need the next one meets.
- **Questions:**
  - What does the reader need to know, and in what sequence?
  - Where is the complication?
  - When should the thesis appear, fully and sharply, given this genre and reader?
  - What's the ending, and does the structure earn it?
- **Techniques:**
  - What / How / Why (Abrams).
  - Pick a pattern from §4.2 by fit, not habit.
  - Know the ending before the final draft.
  - An outline built from the argument, never from source order.
- **Warning signs:**
  - The shuffle test passes (paragraphs are interchangeable).
  - Time words and listing words open the paragraphs.
  - The structure mirrors the sources.
  - Every section has the same shape.
- **Quality criteria:** progressive, not a list; the complication is placed where it bites; the thesis timing is chosen, not accidental.

### Stage 6: Drafting

- **Objective:** get the whole argument down, and discover what you actually think.
- **Questions:** What am I trying to say here? (Orwell's first question, asked of every paragraph.)
- **Techniques:**
  - Draft without self-criticism (Clark).
  - Write toward the ending.
  - Put the claim → evidence → interpretation → implication chain in each paragraph.
  - Anchor each abstraction with a concrete instance next to it.
  - Write the introduction last, or rewrite it last.
- **Warning signs:**
  - Polishing sentences before the shape exists.
  - Forcing the draft to match a thesis it has outgrown.
  - Inflated language (Orwell's "gap between one's real and one's declared aims").
- **Quality criteria:** the complete argument exists on the page; you know whether the thesis survived.

### Stage 7: Evidence

- **Objective:** every contested claim supported at the strength it's asserted.
- **Questions:**
  - Does each claim have enough evidence of the right kind?
  - Is every example representative?
  - Have I said what each piece of evidence shows?
  - Is anything asserted "one iota beyond what I know"?
- **Techniques:**
  - Statistics or mechanism for *that*; cases for *what it's like*.
  - At least as much analysis as evidence.
  - Quote only when the wording matters.
  - In Spitball: corpus grounding, with gaps marked.
- **Warning signs:**
  - Paragraphs of facts with no interpretation.
  - A lone anecdote carrying a general claim.
  - Correlation phrased as causation.
  - Evidence that sits beside the argument instead of inside it.
- **Quality criteria:** a reader could reject your interpretation while accepting your facts, because you kept them visibly apart.

### Stage 8: Revision

- **Objective:** see the draft as a first-time reader would, and fix the big things first.
- **Questions:**
  - Which parts are boring, unconvincing, unclear, or out of order?
  - Where did the argument's shape change during drafting?
- **Techniques:**
  - Passes 1–5 of §12.2, top-down.
  - Reverse outline.
  - Real readers, or a cold reread after time away.
  - The symptom → action table (§12.3).
- **Warning signs:**
  - Only words are changing (Sommers's "thesaurus philosophy").
  - Unconvincing parts are "fixed" with cleverer rhetoric instead of a revised claim.
  - Stopping because no rules are broken.
- **Quality criteria:** the point is where it should be, not where you found it; every paragraph has a statable function; conceptual repetition is gone.

### Stage 9: Final edit

- **Objective:** sentences that deliver the argument without friction, and facts that hold.
- **Questions:**
  - Does every sentence put old information first and emphasis last?
  - Is every passive earning its place?
  - Does every image pass Orwell's freshness question?
  - Is every fact, quote, and attribution checked?
- **Techniques:**
  - Passes 6–10 of §12.2.
  - Read aloud.
  - The first-seven-words test.
  - The paraphrase test for empty sophistication.
  - The style profile's Avoid list.
  - Cut big, then small.
- **Warning signs:**
  - Sentence rhythm on a schedule.
  - Key terms varied for elegance.
  - Hedges on facts, certainty on inferences.
- **Quality criteria:** a smart reader outside the field follows every sentence on the first pass; nothing can be cut without anyone noticing it's gone.

## 17. Article Quality Checklist

Use this before publication. Each item is a yes/no question. A "no" points to the section that explains the fix.

**Idea quality** (§1)
- [ ] Can I state the thesis in one or two sentences?
- [ ] Does it pass the iterated so-what: does a reader bear a cost for not knowing it?
- [ ] Would a representative reader find it neither obvious, irrelevant, nor absurd?
- [ ] Did I learn something by writing this?

**Originality** (§1.3, §9.1)
- [ ] Does it deny at least one assumption the intended reader actually holds?
- [ ] Does it add something the reader couldn't have supplied themselves: a mechanism, distinction, consequence, comparison, or hidden assumption?

**Thesis** (§1.1, §4.3)
- [ ] Is it true, arguable, bounded, and precise, not correct because it's vague?
- [ ] Does it govern the whole piece, with no sections that serve another thesis?
- [ ] Is its placement a choice that fits the genre and reader, not an accident of drafting?

**Reasoning** (§2)
- [ ] Are the contested warrants stated and defended?
- [ ] Does every causal claim name a mechanism or rule out alternatives?
- [ ] Is each generalization proportionate to its sample?
- [ ] Is each analogy relevant to the conclusion, with no critical disanalogy?
- [ ] Is each dichotomy really exhaustive?

**Evidence** (§3)
- [ ] Is every contested claim supported at the strength it's asserted?
- [ ] Is every piece of evidence interpreted: what it shows and why it counts?
- [ ] Is every anecdote representative, or acknowledged not to be?
- [ ] Is the obvious counterevidence addressed?
- [ ] (Spitball) Is every substantive claim grounded in the corpus, with gaps marked instead of filled?

**Intellectual honesty** (§2.3, §9)
- [ ] Is the strongest opposing view stated in a form its holders would accept?
- [ ] Does every objection I raise get an answer?
- [ ] Did I apply the same standard of evidence to views I like and views I don't?
- [ ] Are facts and interpretations visibly separate?
- [ ] Does the confidence match the evidence, flat where sure and precise where not?
- [ ] Does any harsh judgment follow from the analysis rather than replace it?

**Structure** (§4)
- [ ] Does each unit create the need the next one meets (the shuffle test fails)?
- [ ] Does the order follow the argument, not the sources or the retrieval?
- [ ] Is the complication woven in where it bites?
- [ ] Does the reverse outline give every paragraph a statable function, with none that "restates"?

**Paragraphs** (§5)
- [ ] Does the reader know early what each unit is doing?
- [ ] Does each paragraph develop exactly what its opening promised?
- [ ] Do the topic strings hold, with consistent key terms?
- [ ] Does each paragraph end on what matters or on what the next picks up?

**Sentences** (§6)
- [ ] Do subjects name characters or established concepts, and do verbs name the actions?
- [ ] Is each subject near its verb?
- [ ] Is old information first and emphasis last?
- [ ] Does every passive serve the information flow, with none hiding responsibility that matters?

**Clarity** (§11, §12.3)
- [ ] Would a smart reader outside the field follow every sentence on the first pass?
- [ ] Is every abstraction anchored by a concrete instance placed next to it?
- [ ] Has someone other than the writer read it, or has the writer reread it cold?

**Style** (§10, and the named style profile)
- [ ] Does the style serve the thought, with no ornament standing in for a claim?
- [ ] Does every sentence survive the paraphrase test?
- [ ] Does every metaphor carry logic, and is it fresh?
- [ ] Does it follow the style profile, including its Avoid list?

**Engagement** (§7, §11)
- [ ] Does the opening establish motive within the first few sentences?
- [ ] Does the opening's promise match what the piece delivers?
- [ ] Does every vivid detail serve the point, with no seductive asides?
- [ ] Is every question the piece raises actually pursued?

**Conclusion** (§8)
- [ ] Does the ending do new work (implication, reframing, qualification, a final shift) rather than restate?
- [ ] If it returns to the opening, does the return reinterpret it?
- [ ] Is it free of new evidence and unearned zooming out?
